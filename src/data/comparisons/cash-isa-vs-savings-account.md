## The Personal Savings Allowance changed everything

Before 2016, Cash ISAs were essential for anyone earning interest on savings. Then the Personal Savings Allowance (PSA) arrived and made them unnecessary for millions of people.

Basic-rate taxpayers can earn up to £1,000 in savings interest a year tax-free through the PSA. Higher-rate taxpayers get £500. Additional-rate taxpayers get nothing.

At a 4.5% interest rate, a basic-rate taxpayer needs over **{{ 1000 / (4.5 / 100) | roundTo(100) | gbp }}** in savings before they earn more than £1,000 in interest and start paying tax. Most people do not reach that.

## When a Cash ISA makes sense

**Higher-rate taxpayers.** With only £500 of PSA, a higher-rate taxpayer with more than {{ 500 / (4.5 / 100) | roundTo(100) | gbp }} in savings at 4.5% is already paying tax on interest. Moving cash into a Cash ISA removes that.

**Additional-rate taxpayers.** No PSA at all. Every pound of interest is taxed at 45% in 2026-27, rising to 49% from 6 April 2027. A Cash ISA pays from the first penny.

**Large cash balances.** If you keep £30,000 to £50,000 in cash, perhaps [saving for a home deposit](/tools/saving-and-growth/savings-goal/) while holding an [emergency fund](/tools/income-and-planning/emergency-fund/), even a basic-rate taxpayer will exceed the allowance. A Cash ISA shelters the excess.

**Falling rates cut the other way.** If savings rates fall, the PSA covers a larger balance before tax starts, so a Cash ISA helps fewer people.

## When a regular savings account wins

**Most basic-rate taxpayers.** If your savings earn under £1,000 of interest a year, a Cash ISA gives you no tax benefit. In that case choose the account with the best rate, whether it is an ISA or not. Compare current best-buy tables, because either type can come out ahead.

**Bonus and introductory rates.** Some regular savings accounts offer higher rates for a limited time, often with monthly deposit caps. These can beat a Cash ISA for the bonus period.

On £15,000, an extra 0.5 percentage points is {{ 15000 * 0.5 / 100 | gbp }} a year, and if you are within your allowance you keep all of it.

## The real cost: wasting your ISA allowance

This is the point most people miss. Your £20,000 annual ISA allowance is shared across all ISA types: Cash, Stocks & Shares and [Lifetime](/comparisons/lisa-vs-regular-isa/). Every pound in a Cash ISA is a pound not in a [Stocks & Shares ISA](/comparisons/isa-vs-general-investment/).

Over long periods shares have historically returned more than cash, with more risk and no guarantee. The tax shelter is also worth more on investment growth, where you would otherwise pay capital gains tax and dividend tax, than on cash interest, which the PSA already covers for many people.

If you need a Cash ISA for large short-term balances, consider splitting your allowance: keep enough in a Cash ISA to stay under the PSA on your non-ISA savings, and put the rest into Stocks & Shares.

## The numbers for 2026-27

Here is the break-even: the savings balance where interest exceeds your PSA and a Cash ISA starts saving you tax. The table uses an example rate of 4.5%.

| Tax band | PSA | Break-even at 4.5% |
|----------|-----|------------------------|
| Basic rate (20%) | £1,000 | about {{ 1000 / (4.5 / 100) | roundTo(100) | gbp }} |
| Higher rate (40%) | £500 | about {{ 500 / (4.5 / 100) | roundTo(100) | gbp }} |
| Additional rate (45%) | £0 | £0 (any interest is taxed) |

If your savings are below your break-even, a Cash ISA provides no tax advantage. Pick whichever account, ISA or regular, offers the best rate.

**From 6 April 2027, interest above the allowance is taxed more.** The Budget on 28 October 2026 set separate savings tax rates of 24% (basic), 44% (higher) and 49% (additional) from that date, 4 percentage points above the 2026-27 rates. The break-even balances above stay the same, but each £1,000 of interest over your allowance will cost {{ 1000 * 24 / 100 | gbp }}, {{ 1000 * 44 / 100 | gbp }} or {{ 1000 * 49 / 100 | gbp }} in tax instead of {{ 1000 * 20 / 100 | gbp }}, {{ 1000 * 40 / 100 | gbp }} or {{ 1000 * 45 / 100 | gbp }}. If you are already over your allowance, that makes a Cash ISA worth more, not less.

## The bottom line

For basic-rate taxpayers with modest savings, skip the Cash ISA and use the best-paying regular account. Keep your ISA allowance for investments, where the tax shelter is worth more. For higher earners or people with large cash balances, a Cash ISA remains a useful tool, just remember what it costs you in lost investment ISA space. The Cash ISA limit is not being cut: the planned drop to £12,000 was cancelled at the Budget on 28 October 2026, so the whole £20,000 allowance can still go into cash. With savings tax rising from April 2027, higher-rate savers with large cash balances have more reason to use it.
