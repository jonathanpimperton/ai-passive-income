## The rate gap is usually small

The difference between a top high-yield savings account and a CD is usually a fraction of a percentage point, and it moves with the market. On $10,000, a gap of 0.25 points is {{ 10000 * 0.25 / 100 | usd }} a year and a gap of 0.5 points is {{ 10000 * 0.5 / 100 | usd }}. That is worth having, but not worth being unable to reach your money. Check current rates before you decide; they change often and vary a lot between banks.

The real question is whether that small premium is worth giving up access to your money. For most people with money they might need, it is not.

## When high-yield savings accounts win

**Emergency funds.** Your emergency fund must be accessible without a penalty. A CD that penalizes early withdrawal defeats the purpose. Keep [3-6 months of expenses](/tools/income-and-planning/emergency-fund/) in a high-yield savings account.

**Short-term goals (under 12 months).** If you are [saving for a vacation, car or other purchase](/tools/saving-and-growth/savings-goal/) within a year, the flexibility of a savings account outweighs a small CD premium.

**Rising rates.** When the Federal Reserve is raising rates, savings account rates tend to follow, while a CD holds you at the rate you locked in. The Fed raised its target range to 3.75-4.00% on 16 September 2026 and held it there on 28 October, with two members voting for another rise. If you expect rates to keep climbing, keep the money in a savings account or a short CD so you can move up as rates do.

## When CDs win

**Rates are expected to fall.** If you expect rate cuts, locking in a rate for 12-24 months guarantees your return while savings account rates decline. When the Fed began cutting in September 2024, people holding CDs locked at earlier, higher rates kept them while savings rates fell.

**Money you will not need for a set period.** [Saving a home deposit](/scenarios/save-for-house-deposit/) you need in 18 months? A CD maturing on that date locks in your rate and removes the temptation to spend it.

**CD laddering.** Spread your money across CDs maturing at different intervals, for example 3, 6, 9, 12 and 18 months. As each matures, reinvest it in a new, longer CD. You get regular access to a portion of your money while capturing longer-term rates. As an illustration with example rates (not quotes): on $50,000 split into five $10,000 rungs earning 3.75%, 3.90%, 4.05%, 4.25% and 4.50%, the first year's interest is {{ 10000 * (3.75 + 3.9 + 4.05 + 4.25 + 4.5) / 100 | usd }}, assuming maturing rungs roll into new CDs at similar rates.

## The penalty problem

CD early withdrawal penalties are often several months of interest, and longer terms usually carry larger penalties. If you break a 12-month CD after 4 months, a 6-month penalty can exceed everything you earned, as the table above shows: {{ 10000 * 4.25 / 100 * 6 / 12 | usd }} against {{ 10000 * 4.25 / 100 * 4 / 12 | usd }}. In that case you would have been better off in a savings account. Read the penalty terms before you buy.

Some banks offer no-penalty CDs, but these usually pay less than standard CDs, sometimes no more than a high-yield savings account, which removes their main advantage.

## What about money market accounts?

[Money market accounts](/comparisons/emergency-fund-savings-vs-money-market/) sit between savings and CDs. Their rates are usually close to high-yield savings rates: some banks pay a little more on a money market account and others a little less. They stay fully liquid, and the trade-off is usually a higher minimum balance.

For a large emergency fund a money market account can be a good middle ground, but the difference is small enough that the best advice is to pick the highest-rate option that gives you the access you need.

## What about Treasury bills?

Treasury bills are another cash option. You can buy them at auction through TreasuryDirect or through most brokerages, in terms from 4 to 52 weeks. Yields change with each auction, so compare the current yield with the best savings and CD rates.

Their distinctive advantage is tax: T-bill interest is exempt from state and local income tax, though not from federal tax. In a high-tax state like California or New York that exemption is worth extra yield. At a 4.2% T-bill yield and a 9% state rate, you keep about {{ 4.2 * 9 / 100 | num(2) }} percentage points that savings interest would lose to the state. In a state with no income tax the advantage disappears.

The trade-off mirrors CDs: your money is committed until maturity, though terms are shorter and there is a liquid secondary market if you need to sell early.
