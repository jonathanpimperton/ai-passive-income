## $1 million supports about $40,000 a year from age 55

**Under the widely used 4% rule, $1 million supports withdrawals of about $40,000 a year ($3,333 a month) before tax. A more cautious 3.5% rate, often suggested for retirements that may last 35 to 40 years, gives about $35,000.** Whether that is enough depends on your spending and on how you cover the years before Social Security and Medicare begin; both are covered below.

Getting there is realistic. **Starting with $50,000 at age 30 and contributing $1,500 a month at a 7% average annual return (compounded monthly), you would have about $1,501,378 by age 55**, comfortably past the million-dollar mark.

## Growth from age 30 to 55: contributions vs. compounding

Over 25 years, you contribute a total of $500,000 out of pocket ($1,500 x 300 months plus your initial $50,000). The remaining $1,001,378 comes from investment returns: by 55, two-thirds of the balance is growth.

Here is how the balance grows over time:

- **By age 35 (5 years in):** ~$178,300 — your $50k start plus steady contributions are building a foundation.
- **By age 40 (10 years in):** ~$360,100 — you cross the $300k mark as compounding starts to accelerate.
- **By age 45 (15 years in):** ~$617,900 — investment gains now make up almost half (48%) of the balance.
- **By age 50 (20 years in):** ~$983,300 — the final stretch sees the fastest growth.
- **By age 55 (25 years in):** ~$1,501,400 — you pass your target with room to spare.

The million-dollar question is not just whether you can accumulate $1 million — it is whether $1 million will sustain you for 30 or more years of retirement.

## The hidden costs of retiring a decade early

The widely cited **4% rule** suggests you can safely withdraw 4% of your portfolio each year without running out of money over a 30-year retirement. On the $1.5 million projected above, that gives you approximately **$60,055 per year** ($5,005/month) before taxes.

But retiring at 55 creates unique challenges that traditional retirees at 65 do not face:

- **Social Security gap:** You cannot claim benefits until age 62 (reduced) or 67 (full). That is 7 to 12 years of living entirely off savings.
- **Healthcare costs:** Medicare does not start until 65. Marketplace insurance for a 55-year-old runs $600 to $1,200 per month in most states. Budget $7,200 to $14,400 per year for a decade.
- **Early withdrawal penalties:** Pulling from a 401(k) or traditional IRA before 59 and a half triggers a 10% penalty plus income tax. A **Roth conversion ladder** (converting traditional IRA funds to Roth five years before you need them) or **Rule of 55** (penalty-free 401(k) withdrawal if you leave your job at 55 or later) can help you avoid this.
- **Longer timeline risk:** A 55-year-old retiree needs their money to last 35 to 40 years, not 25 to 30. Some financial planners recommend using a 3.5% withdrawal rate for early retirees, which drops your annual spending on $1.5 million to ~$52,500 (or $35,000 on exactly $1 million).

A bridge strategy is essential: keep 3 to 5 years of expenses in cash or short-term bonds to cover the gap before Social Security and Medicare kick in, while the rest stays invested for long-term growth.

## Adjusting the savings rate, timeline, and return assumptions

- **You only save $1,000/month instead of $1,500:** Your balance at 55 drops to approximately $1,096,300, still above the $1 million target but with much less room for a bad decade in the markets.
- **You start at 25 instead of 30 (same $50,000 and $1,500/month):** Five extra years of compounding grows your balance to roughly $2,235,800. Those five early years are worth over $700,000.
- **Markets return 5% instead of 7%:** Your ending balance falls to approximately $1,067,300. A 2% difference in returns costs you over $430,000, a reminder that fees and asset allocation matter enormously over long time horizons.
- **You increase contributions by $100 each year:** Gradually ramping from $1,500 to $3,900 per month over 25 years would push your balance to about $2.19 million, giving you a 4% withdrawal of about $87,700 per year.
