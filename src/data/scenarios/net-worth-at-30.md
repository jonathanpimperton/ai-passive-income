## The average is $135,400, but the median is only $33,000

**According to the Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF, published 9 October 2026), US families headed by someone under 35 have a mean (average) net worth of $135,400 and a median of $33,000.** The gap between mean and median tells you that a small number of high-net-worth families pull the average up. For most 30-year-olds, $33,000 is the more relevant benchmark.

Young families lost ground in the latest survey. The under-35 median fell 23% from the 2022 survey and the mean fell 33%, both in inflation-adjusted (2025) dollars. The Fed says the declines among young families are primarily driven by the unwinding of business equity gains between 2019 and 2022. In 2025 dollars the 2022 figures were a $42,700 median and a $200,800 mean.

Source: [Federal Reserve, "Changes in U.S. Family Finances from 2022 to 2025: Evidence from the Survey of Consumer Finances" (Oct 2026), Table 2](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf26.pdf). The SCF is run every three years, so this stays the latest figure until the next survey is published.

## What net worth actually measures

Net worth = total assets minus total liabilities.

**Assets include:**
- Retirement accounts (401(k), IRA): Often the largest asset for someone in their late 20s/early 30s
- Bank accounts (checking + savings)
- Investment accounts (brokerage, crypto)
- Home equity (market value minus mortgage balance)
- Car value
- Other property

**Liabilities include:**
- Student loans
- Car loans
- Credit card balances
- Mortgage balance
- Personal loans

A 30-year-old with $60,000 in a 401(k), $15,000 in savings, $45,000 in student loans, and no home has a net worth of {{ 60000 + 15000 - 45000 | usd }}. That is {{ 33000 - (60000 + 15000 - 45000) | usd }} below the $33,000 median for under-35s. The calculator's example inputs on this page ($120,000 of assets and $45,000 of liabilities) give {{ 120000 - 45000 | usd }}, which is above the median.

## Common benchmarks and rules of thumb

Several personal finance frameworks offer net worth targets by age:

**The "1x salary by 30" rule (Fidelity):**
Fidelity's guideline is to have saved at least 1x your salary in retirement savings by 30. If you earn $55,000, that is $55,000. Fidelity calls the milestones aspirational and assumes you save 15% of your income, employer match included, starting at 25 and retire at 67 ([Fidelity: how much do I need to retire](https://www.fidelity.com/viewpoints/retirement/how-much-do-i-need-to-retire)). It is a retirement-savings target, not a net worth target.

**The Millionaire Next Door formula:**
Expected net worth = (age × pre-tax income) ÷ 10. At 30 with $55,000 income: {{ 30 * 55000 / 10 | usd }}. That is {{ 30 * 55000 / 10 / 33000 | num(1) }} times the SCF median, and few 30-year-olds reach it. The formula works better for older age groups.

**A practical benchmark:**
Having any positive net worth at 30 puts you ahead of many peers who are still paying down student loans. If your net worth is above the $33,000 median, you are in the top half for your age group.

## Why most 30-year-olds have low net worth

Several factors compress net worth for under-35s:

- **Student debt:** Balances for advanced degrees can reach six figures, and even a bachelor's degree often leaves tens of thousands of dollars owed. This alone can make net worth negative well into your 30s.
- **Short earning history:** Most people have only 5–8 years of full-time earnings by age 30, and entry-level salaries are the lowest of your career.
- **Housing costs:** In expensive cities, rent absorbs a large share of take-home pay, leaving little for savings.
- **Late start on retirement savings:** Many people do not start contributing to a 401(k) until their mid-to-late 20s.

Having a negative net worth at 30, which is common if you have student loans and no home equity, does not mean you are failing. It means you are in the early accumulation phase.

## How to grow net worth from here

The biggest drivers of net worth growth between 30 and 40:

1. **Increase retirement contributions.** Contributing the 2026 401(k) limit of {{ usFact('k401Limit', year=2026) | usd }} a year for 10 years at an assumed 7% return grows to about {{ compoundFV(0, usFact('k401Limit', year=2026) / 12, 7, 10) | roundTo(1000) | usd }}, assuming the same contribution every month and no employer match. For anyone who can afford it, that is usually the largest single lever on net worth. See [IRS: 401(k) limit increases to $24,500 for 2026](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500).
2. **Pay down high-interest debt.** Every dollar of credit card debt eliminated adds $1 to net worth, and saves 20%+ a year in interest if that is your card's rate.
3. **Build an emergency fund.** A cash buffer prevents debt-funded emergencies from destroying progress ([6-month emergency fund](/scenarios/6-month-emergency-fund/)).
4. **Increase income.** Job changes in your early 30s often come with larger raises than staying put, which creates more room to save and invest.

The decade between 30 and 40 is typically the fastest net worth growth period for most people, as incomes rise, debts get paid down, and compound interest begins to work. The [$1 million by 40](/scenarios/net-worth-millionaire-by-40/) page shows what it takes to reach seven figures.
